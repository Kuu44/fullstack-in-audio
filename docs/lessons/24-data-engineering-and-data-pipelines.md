# Chapter 24 — Data engineering and data pipelines

> Narration script. Headings, frontmatter, and blockquotes are stripped before rendering; only the prose below is spoken.

Welcome to chapter twenty-four. This is the first chapter of Part Five, and it is a turning point in the course. For the last several chapters you have been building intelligence: prompts, tools, agents, retrieval, evaluations, and a cost and latency budget. All of that work rests on an assumption we have never examined. The assumption is that the data your system reasons over is correct, current, and complete. In this chapter we take that assumption apart and replace it with something you can defend to a customer.

Here is the situation in the FieldOps Copilot build. An operator reports an incident. Your service records it, your agent proposes a triage, and retrieval grounds that proposal in the customer's runbooks. But a real incident is about a real thing: a server, a badge reader, a chiller unit, a point-of-sale terminal, a warehouse conveyor. That thing lives in a system you do not own. It might be a configuration management database, an asset register in a facilities platform, a spreadsheet that a regional manager updates on Fridays, or an export from an enterprise resource planning system. Whatever it is, the customer already has it, it is already imperfect, and your copilot's usefulness depends on it.

So today we build the path from their system to yours. In the field, that path is usually the least glamorous part of a deployment and the most common reason a pilot quietly fails. When an operator tells you the copilot gave a wrong answer, the root cause is far more often a stale, malformed, or duplicated record than a defect in the model. I want you to leave this chapter treating the data path as a first-class part of the product, with its own contract, its own tests, its own failure reporting, and its own place in the architecture diagram.

Let us start with vocabulary, because data engineering has a lot of it and most of it is simpler than it sounds.

A source system is any system of record you read from and do not control. The defining property is not the technology. It is the ownership. Somebody else decides when it changes, what a field means, and whether today's export ran. Every assumption you make about a source system is a risk you are carrying on the customer's behalf, so the first job of a data engineer is to write those assumptions down where somebody can challenge them.

Ingestion is the act of pulling records out of the source and getting them into your boundary. It has two broad shapes. In a pull model, your system reaches out on a schedule and asks for records: an application programming interface call, a database query, a file drop you poll. In a push model, the source sends records to you when something changes: a webhook, a message on a queue, a file delivered to a location you watch. Pull is easier to reason about because you control when it happens and you can repeat it. Push gives you lower latency but makes you responsible for accepting whatever arrives, whenever it arrives, including twice.

Extract, transform, and load describes the three verbs of a pipeline. The order in which you do them gives you two common patterns. In the older pattern, you extract from the source, transform the data in flight, and load the finished shape into the destination. In the newer pattern, you extract, load the raw records into your destination almost untouched, and then transform inside the destination. The newer pattern has a real advantage for field work: because you kept the raw form, you can reprocess history when you discover your transform was wrong, and you will discover that. The older pattern uses less storage and gives you a smaller destination, which matters if the customer is paying per gigabyte or if their policy limits what you may retain.

A schema contract is a written, checkable statement of what a record must look like for you to accept it. It names the fields you require, the type and shape of each one, the values that are allowed, which field identifies the record uniquely, and what you do when any of that is violated. The word contract matters. It is a two-party agreement, not a private validation rule. When a source team changes a field and your pipeline rejects their records, the contract is what turns an argument into a conversation.

Normalization is the step where you convert an accepted record from the source's vocabulary into yours. The source might call a field asset tag; you call it asset identifier. The source might record criticality as the words high, medium, and low, or as the numbers one through five, or as a mix of both because two teams merged three years ago. Normalization is where you decide, once, in one place, what those become inside FieldOps Copilot.

Idempotency means that running the same operation more than once has the same effect as running it once. This is the single most valuable property in a pipeline, and it is worth saying slowly. If your ingestion job is idempotent, then a retry is safe, a partial failure is safe, a nervous operator clicking run again is safe, and a backfill over last month is safe. If it is not idempotent, every one of those becomes an incident, and the damage is usually duplicate records that nobody notices until a report is wrong.

Lineage is the ability to answer, for any record in your system, where it came from and when. At the scale of a pilot, lineage is not a fancy graph product. It is a handful of columns on every row you load: which source produced it, which revision or export of that source, when you ingested it, and which run of the job did the work. Those columns cost almost nothing and they are the difference between diagnosing a data problem in ten minutes and arguing about it for a week.

A dead-letter queue, or more plainly a quarantine, is where a record goes when you cannot accept it. The critical design idea is that rejecting a record is not the same as losing it. A quarantined record keeps its original content, gains a reason for rejection, and becomes a visible, countable thing that somebody can act on.

Finally, the distinction that organizes everything else: operational data versus analytical data. Your PostgreSQL incident table is operational. It serves live requests, it must be correct right now, it is small, it is written by your application, and it is the system of record for work in progress. Analytical data is the accumulated history you reason over: every incident ever, joined to assets, joined to outcomes, sliced by month. It tolerates being minutes or hours behind, it grows without bound, and it is read by reports, evaluations, and models. Mixing the two is one of the most reliable ways to make a customer system slow and fragile, because a single unbounded analytical query will happily consume the resources your operators need to file an incident.

That leads to the shape we want for FieldOps Copilot. The incident record stays where it is, in the operational database, authoritative and transactional. The asset inventory arrives through a pipeline and lands in what we will call a read model: a table that exists to be read by the application, that your application never treats as the truth, and that you can drop and rebuild from the source at any time. That last property is your safety net. If you can rebuild it, you can fix it.

Now, why should a forward deployed engineer care about all of this more than a typical application engineer?

The first reason is that in the field you inherit data you did not create. In a product company, the data your application reasons over was produced by your application, under your schema, with your validation. In customer delivery, the most important data is decades old, was entered by humans under time pressure, has been migrated between three systems, and contains categories that exist for reasons nobody present can explain. You will find assets with no owner, owners who left the company, criticality ratings that were set once during a compliance push in a prior fiscal year, and duplicate entries that differ by a trailing space. None of that is a reason to refuse the project. It is the project.

The second reason is that data problems arrive disguised as product problems. Watch how this plays out. Your copilot suggests routing a critical incident to a team that was dissolved. The operator sees a wrong answer from an artificial intelligence system. The manager hears that the artificial intelligence is unreliable. The security lead hears that it is making up team names. By the time it reaches you it has become a credibility problem, and no amount of prompt tuning will fix it, because the routing table was exported before the reorganization. If your pipeline had recorded when it last successfully refreshed, and your agent had said that its asset context was eleven days old, that entire chain of damage would have been one sentence in the interface.

The third reason is that the customer's data owner is a stakeholder you must recruit early. Somebody in that organization is accountable for the asset inventory. They have opinions about who may read it, how often it may be queried, and what happens if your job hammers their interface at nine in the morning. If the first time they hear about your pipeline is when it breaks something, your pilot has acquired an opponent. If you go to them in week one with a written contract, a stated read frequency, and a question about which fields they consider reliable, you usually acquire an ally, and often a warning about the three fields you were about to trust that nobody maintains.

The fourth reason is cost and blast radius. Reading a customer's production system is an operational act. A poorly bounded query against an enterprise resource planning database during month-end close is a genuine incident with your name on it. Field work means designing extraction to be small, scheduled considerately, bounded in time range, and easy to switch off.

Let me make this concrete with a failure I want you to be able to prevent. Picture a deployment three weeks into a pilot. The copilot has been well received. One morning an operator files an incident about a refrigeration unit at a distribution site. The copilot enriches it with asset context, sees a criticality of low, proposes a routine queue, and the operator accepts because the copilot has been right for three weeks. The unit is in fact the one that holds temperature-sensitive stock, and it was reclassified as critical two months ago. Nobody notices for nine hours.

Now trace backwards through the causes, because every one of them is a design decision you get to make today. The reclassification happened in the customer's facilities platform. That platform exports a nightly file. The export job started failing eleven days ago because a certificate expired on the file transfer host. Your ingestion job ran on schedule every one of those eleven nights, found the same file sitting in the same place, ingested it successfully, reported success, and updated nothing, because the file had not changed. Every individual record in that file was valid. Your record-level validation passed with a perfect score, every night, on data that was frozen.

Count the missing controls. There was no freshness assertion comparing the source's own modified timestamps against the clock, so a frozen file looked identical to a stable one. There was no row-level or batch-level change detection, so eleven identical loads raised no question. The read model exposed no last-refreshed value, so neither the interface nor the agent could mention its own age. The agent's instructions did not require it to qualify asset context, so it spoke about criticality with the same confidence it uses for facts it actually knows. And no alert existed for a feed that stops changing, only for a feed that stops arriving.

Notice what is not on that list. The model was fine. The prompt was fine. Retrieval was fine. The service was up the whole time, the dashboard was green, and the system produced a confidently wrong answer for eleven days. That is the characteristic shape of a data failure in the field, and it is why I am asking you to spend a full chapter on plumbing. Every control I describe in the rest of this lesson exists to make some version of that morning impossible or, at minimum, obvious within a day.

So let us design the thing. I will walk through the pipeline as a sequence of decisions, and you will implement your own version in the practice test.

Decision one is the contract. Before you write any ingestion code, write the contract for a customer asset. For FieldOps Copilot, I want four required fields and a small number of optional ones. Required: a stable asset identifier, an owning team, a service or location the asset belongs to, and a criticality. Optional but useful: a human-readable name, a status such as active or retired, and a last-verified date from the source. For each required field, state the type, the allowed values if it is an enumeration, and whether an empty value is a rejection or a defaulted value. Criticality deserves special attention because it feeds your triage priority. Decide the exact mapping from the source's vocabulary to yours and decide what an unrecognized value means. My recommendation is that an unrecognized criticality is a rejection, not a silent default to medium, because a silent default is indistinguishable from a real medium and it will quietly corrupt your priority distribution.

Decision two is the identity of a record. Which field makes an asset the same asset across two exports? Usually it is the asset identifier from the source system. Write down what makes it stable, and write down what happens if the source reuses an identifier after retiring an asset. If the source has no stable identifier, you must construct one from a combination of fields, and you must accept that your constructed key will change if any component changes, which means yesterday's asset will look like a new asset tomorrow. That is a real limitation to disclose to the customer rather than hide in the code.

Decision three is how you detect change. The simplest approach is a full snapshot: each run you read every record, and the result replaces what you had. Full snapshots are wonderfully easy to reason about and they are naturally idempotent, so use them whenever the volume allows. The alternative is incremental loading, where you only fetch records changed since a marker, usually a timestamp you store from the previous successful run. That marker is called a watermark. Incremental loading is cheaper and faster, but it has three well-known traps. First, records modified during your run can be missed, which you mitigate by overlapping the window slightly and relying on idempotency. Second, deletions are invisible: if an asset is removed from the source, an incremental feed simply stops mentioning it, and your copy keeps it forever unless the source provides a deletion signal. Third, the source's modified timestamp may be maintained inconsistently, and you will only learn that by comparing an incremental run against a full snapshot. For a pilot, my strong advice is to start with a full snapshot, and only move to incremental loading when you have measured that the snapshot is actually too expensive.

Before decision four, a short detour on cadence, because people reach for streaming far too early. There are three broad rhythms available to you. Scheduled batch runs on a clock: once a night, once an hour, every fifteen minutes. Triggered batch runs when something says there is new work: a file landed, a webhook fired, an operator pressed refresh. Streaming processes records continuously as they occur, with no batch boundary at all.

The right choice comes from one question, and it is not a technical question. How out of date can this data be before it changes a decision a human makes? For an asset inventory, the honest answer is usually hours. Assets are not reclassified every minute. A nightly refresh with a mid-day top-up is almost always sufficient, and it costs a fraction of what a streaming integration costs to build and to operate. For incident status coming back from a customer's ticketing system, the tolerance might be minutes, which argues for triggered batch on a webhook with a scheduled reconciliation to catch what the webhook missed. Streaming earns its place when the tolerance is seconds and the volume is continuous, which in an incident triage pilot is almost never true.

I want you to write the tolerance down as a number, because that number is the requirement. It tells you the schedule. It tells you the freshness threshold at which your read model should declare itself stale. It tells you how urgent a failed run is, and therefore whether the alert wakes somebody up or waits for the morning. And it gives you something to renegotiate with the customer when they ask for real time, because real time is not a requirement, it is a budget request. Ask what decision changes if the data is thirty minutes old. Frequently the answer is nothing, and you have just saved the pilot a week of work and an ongoing operational burden.

One more thing about cadence. Whatever schedule you choose, make the job runnable on demand by a human with the same code path. During a customer visit you will want to refresh the inventory while somebody watches, and you do not want that to be a different, less tested route into your system.

Decision four is the anatomy of the job. I want you to build it as four distinct stages with a clear boundary between each one, because that boundary is what makes the pipeline testable and what makes a failure legible.

The first stage is extract. It talks to the source, and it does nothing else. It does not validate, it does not reshape, it does not decide. Its output is the raw records exactly as received, plus metadata about the fetch: when it started, what range it asked for, how many records came back, and any revision or version identifier the source gave you. Keeping extract dumb means you can replay a transform against yesterday's raw data without touching the customer's system again, which you will want more often than you expect.

The second stage is validate. It applies the contract to each record and sorts them into two piles: accepted and rejected. A rejected record carries the reason, the field at fault, and enough of the original to be recognizable. Two things matter here. First, validation is per record, not per batch, so one bad row does not discard nine hundred good ones. Second, there must be a limit: if more than some proportion of the batch fails, that is not a set of bad records, it is a broken source or a changed schema, and the correct response is to stop the run and raise an alert rather than to load a fraction of the data. Pick that threshold deliberately and write it down. Something around ten percent is a reasonable starting point for an asset inventory.

The third stage is normalize and transform. Accepted records get converted to your vocabulary. Trim whitespace, standardize case where it is safe, map criticality, resolve owner names to your team representation, and attach lineage fields. This is also where you handle duplicates within a single batch: if two rows share an identity key, decide the rule now. Last one wins, most recently modified wins, or reject both as ambiguous. Any of those can be correct; silently keeping both is not.

The fourth stage is load. Write the normalized records into the read model in a way that is safe to repeat. The standard technique is an upsert keyed on your identity field: insert the record if it is new, update it if you have seen it. With a full snapshot you can go further and load into a fresh staging area, verify the count and a few quality assertions, and then swap it into place in a single transaction. That gives readers either the complete old version or the complete new version, never a half-loaded table. If you cannot swap, at minimum do the upsert inside a transaction and mark records that were absent from a full snapshot as no longer present rather than deleting them outright.

Now let us talk about the quality checks that sit alongside those stages, because validation of individual records is not sufficient. Record-level validation catches a malformed row. It does not catch the batch that arrived with three hundred rows when yesterday had nine thousand, which usually means an export filtered on the wrong date. It does not catch the run where every criticality suddenly became the same value. It does not catch the feed that has not changed at all in two weeks because an upstream job has been silently failing. So add a small number of batch-level assertions: an expected row-count range compared against recent runs, a check that key fields are not overwhelmingly null, a check that the distribution of criticality has not collapsed, and a freshness check on the source's own modified timestamps. Three or four assertions are enough for a pilot and they catch the failures that actually happen.

Next, lineage and run metadata. Every row you load should carry the source name, the source revision or export identifier, the ingestion timestamp, and the job run identifier. Every run should produce a record of its own: when it started and ended, how many records were extracted, accepted, rejected, inserted, and updated, which assertions passed, and the final status. That run record is not bureaucracy. It is the thing you open first when somebody asks why the copilot said something strange on Tuesday, and it is the evidence you hand the customer when they ask whether the integration is healthy.

A word on what happens to quarantined records after you quarantine them, because a quarantine nobody reads is just a slower way of dropping data. Rejected records need an owner and a rhythm. In practice, the reasons sort into three groups, and each group goes to a different person. Some rejections are contract bugs on your side: you required a field the source legitimately leaves blank, or your allowed values missed a category that has existed for years. Those are yours to fix, and the fix is to amend the contract, not to loosen validation until everything passes. Some rejections are genuine data-quality problems in the source: an asset with no owner, a criticality nobody ever set. Those belong to the customer's data owner, and your job is to hand them a short, readable list rather than a stack trace. And some rejections are a schema change nobody told you about, which is a conversation, and the fastest way to have it well is to point at the contract you agreed on in week one.

So give the quarantine a small amount of structure: the reason, the field, the count, and when it first appeared. A count matters more than you would think. Three rejected assets out of nine thousand is a footnote. Three hundred that appeared this week is a signal. And put the count somewhere visible during the pilot, even if that is just a line in your weekly status note, because an integration whose error rate is reported regularly is one the customer trusts, and a silent integration is one they eventually stop believing.

Then the join. The whole point of this work is that an incident can be enriched with asset context. Define the join narrowly. An incident references an asset by the same identifier the contract defines, and nothing else. Resist the temptation to match on names, because fuzzy matching on a customer's asset names will look brilliant in a demo and will produce confidently wrong context in production. If the operator's intake form allows free text for the affected service, then the correct design is to resolve that text to an asset identifier at intake time, show the operator what you matched, and let them correct it. That way the ambiguity is resolved by a human at the moment they have the most context, and everything downstream deals in identifiers.

Which brings us to the most important behavior in this entire chapter: the difference between no asset found and the pipeline data is stale. These are completely different situations and your agent must be able to tell them apart. No asset found means the lookup succeeded and the identifier is genuinely unknown, which might mean the operator mistyped it or the asset is new. Stale means your read model has not been refreshed within its expected window, so an absence proves nothing and a present record may be out of date. Your read model should expose its own freshness, your lookup tool should return that freshness alongside any result, and your agent's instructions should require it to say when its asset context is old. In the interface, an operator should be able to see that the asset inventory was last refreshed at a particular time. This single feature converts a mysterious wrong answer into an obvious operational task, and it is the kind of thing customers remember about a delivery.

Let me now go through the pitfalls I see most often, because knowing the shape of a mistake is faster than rediscovering it.

The first pitfall is the invisible partial failure. A job catches every exception, logs a friendly message, and exits successfully with half the data loaded. Everything looks green and the data is wrong. Make your job fail loudly. A run that could not complete its contract must end in a failed state, and a failed state must be visible somewhere a human looks.

The second pitfall is transforming inside the extract. Somebody adds a small cleanup to the fetch because it was convenient. Six weeks later nobody can reproduce a historical load because the raw data was never kept. Keep the boundary.

The third pitfall is validation that only exists in the loader. If your only schema enforcement is a database constraint, then a violation produces a database error that is hard to attribute and impossible to quarantine gracefully. Validate against the contract before you get to the database, and let the database constraint be your second line of defense rather than your first.

The fourth pitfall is the silent default. Any time you fill in a missing value with a plausible one, you have created a record that is indistinguishable from real data. If you must default, mark the record as defaulted so a human can later ask how many of these exist.

The fifth pitfall is treating the read model as the source of truth. Somebody writes a feature that updates an asset record in your table. Now the next refresh silently reverts their change and nobody understands why. The read model must be read-only to the application. If the customer needs to edit asset data, that edit belongs in their system, or in a separate override table that your pipeline deliberately respects.

The sixth pitfall is unbounded extraction. A query with no time bound, no row limit, and no timeout against a production system is an outage waiting for a busy morning. Bound it, time it, and make it easy to disable.

The seventh pitfall is ignoring deletions. If the source removes an asset and you never notice, your copilot keeps offering context for equipment that no longer exists. With a full snapshot, absence is your deletion signal, and you should mark records absent rather than delete them, so you retain the history and can explain what happened.

The eighth pitfall is running the pipeline against real customer data before anyone approved it. This is the one that ends pilots. Until you have written approval for a specific data flow, work with a synthetic inventory that you generate yourself. It should be realistic in shape, including the messy parts, and entirely invented in content.

There is a ninth pitfall that is organizational rather than technical, and it causes more grief than any of the others. It is the pipeline nobody owns after you leave. During the pilot you are present, so when the feed breaks you notice it within hours because you are watching. After handover, the question is who gets told, and what they are expected to do. If the answer is nobody, then the system degrades invisibly over months in exactly the way the refrigeration story described. So decide during the build, not at the end: which signal indicates a broken feed, where that signal appears, who is accountable for acting on it, and what the fallback behavior is while it is broken. The fallback is the part people forget. A copilot whose asset context is unavailable should still accept incidents and still triage them, marking its proposals as lacking asset context, rather than failing intake. Degrading a feature is acceptable. Blocking the operator's primary job because a nightly export failed is not.

Now, verification. How do you know the pipeline you are about to build actually works? Not by watching it succeed once. Here are the checks I want you to be able to demonstrate.

Run the same source twice without changing it. The second run must leave your read model in exactly the same state as the first, with the same number of rows. If the count changes, your load is not idempotent and you have found the bug before the customer did.

Introduce a record that violates the contract in one specific way, such as an unrecognized criticality, and confirm it appears in quarantine with a reason a non-engineer can read. Then confirm every other record in that batch still loaded.

Introduce a duplicate identity key inside a single batch and confirm your stated rule applied, rather than producing two rows.

Add a column to the source that your contract does not know about, and confirm the run still succeeds. Unknown extra fields should be tolerated and ignored, because sources add columns constantly and a pilot that breaks on every upstream addition is a pilot that needs babysitting. The inverse is not true: a missing required field must be a rejection.

Remove a record from the source and confirm your read model marks it absent rather than keeping it silently forever.

Set the clock forward, or just fake the last successful refresh time, and confirm your lookup reports the data as stale and your agent's response acknowledges that.

Finally, look at your run record and confirm you can state, from that record alone, how many rows were accepted and rejected and why the run ended the way it did.

Here is your practice handoff. The test in the companion guide asks you to ingest customer asset context safely into FieldOps Copilot. You will define a synthetic asset inventory source and write a contract for it. You will build an ingestion job with distinct extract, validate, transform, and load stages that is safe to run repeatedly. You will record lineage on every row and produce a run record for every execution. You will join assets to incidents through the defined identifier only. You will deliberately break the source in at least two ways, an unexpected column and a duplicate record, and show what your pipeline did about it. And you will make your agent able to distinguish an unknown asset from stale data.

Do not build a general-purpose data platform. One source, one contract, one read model, four stages, a quarantine, and a run record. The discipline is in the boundaries, not the breadth.

Let me recap the decisions that matter. Data in a customer deployment arrives from systems you do not own, so write a contract before you write a fetch. Keep extract, validate, transform, and load as separate stages so that failures are attributable and transforms are replayable. Make loading idempotent, because retries and backfills are normal, not exceptional. Quarantine bad records with readable reasons instead of dropping them or crashing the run. Carry lineage on every row and produce a run record for every execution. Add a few batch-level assertions, because the failures that hurt are the ones where every individual record looks fine. Keep your analytical read model separate from your operational system of record, and never let the application write to the read model. And above all, teach your system to know when its own data is stale, because a copilot that admits its asset context is eleven days old is trustworthy, and one that confidently answers from an eleven-day-old snapshot is not.
